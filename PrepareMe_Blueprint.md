# PrepareMe: Founder-Grade Blueprint
### The AI Learning Operating System for Institutions

---

## 1. Executive Summary

PrepareMe is not an AI tutor. It is not a chatbot on top of a curriculum PDF. It is not a quiz generator.

PrepareMe is an **institutional learning operating system** — a platform that ingests a school's or university's curriculum, converts it into a proprietary knowledge graph, tracks every student's mastery over time, governs all AI outputs against approved content, and gives teachers and administrators a control plane that compounds in value the longer they use it.

The core thesis: **the moat is not the model. The moat is the data structure, the memory, the workflow, and the institutional lock-in that builds underneath the AI layer.**

When the LLM changes — and it will — PrepareMe's curriculum graphs, student mastery histories, institutional policies, and teacher workflows remain intact. The product gets smarter every month not because the model improves, but because the institution's data deepens.

The target customer is mid-to-large educational institutions — universities, coaching academies, EdTech platforms, and K-12 school chains — that have serious curriculum assets, real exam pressures, and no AI infrastructure of their own.

The wedge is exam preparation. The expansion is the full learning operating system. The endgame is becoming the institutional layer that every school builds on top of.

**The business opportunity:** The global EdTech market exceeds $400B. AI-native tools for institutions are nascent. The window to own the curriculum-intelligence layer before incumbents like Canvas, Blackboard, or Google Classroom bolt on shallow AI features is open — but narrowing fast.

## 2. Product Category Thesis

### The Category: Curriculum-Aware Institutional AI

Today's AI tools for education fall into two failure modes:

**Failure Mode 1 — The Chatbot Wrapper**
A generic LLM with a system prompt that says "you are a helpful tutor." No curriculum awareness. No memory. No governance. One jailbreak away from hallucinating an exam answer.

**Failure Mode 2 — The Feature Layer**
A quiz generator, a flashcard app, a summarizer. Useful in isolation. No integration into the institution's curriculum. No compounding value. Replaceable in a weekend.

PrepareMe creates a third category: **Curriculum-Aware Institutional AI** — a platform where:
- Every AI response is grounded in the institution's approved materials
- Every student interaction is remembered and drives future recommendations
- Every teacher gets a control plane, not just a chat window
- Every institution builds a proprietary knowledge asset over time

This is closer to how Salesforce owns CRM or how Workday owns HR workflows — not by being the best AI, but by becoming the system of record that makes switching painful and expensive.

### Why This Category Wins

Generic AI assistants cannot serve institutions because institutions need:
1. **Accountability** — "Why did the AI say that?" must have a traceable answer
2. **Curriculum fidelity** — answers must come from approved material, not the internet
3. **Governance** — teachers and administrators must be able to review, approve, and override
4. **Memory** — the system must know what each student has learned, missed, and forgotten
5. **Auditability** — for regulators, accreditors, and parents

None of these are properties of a chatbot. All of them are properties of a platform.

## 5. Moat Architecture

The moat is layered. Each layer independently creates retention. Together, they create a system that is very difficult to exit and nearly impossible to replicate quickly.

### Layer 1: Curriculum Graph (Data Moat)
Every institution that onboards PrepareMe contributes to building a proprietary, machine-readable curriculum graph — a structured representation of their entire academic program: courses, units, topics, subtopics, learning outcomes, prerequisites, difficulty weights, exam coverage, and content provenance.

This graph takes weeks to build correctly. It encodes institutional knowledge that exists nowhere else. It is not exportable in any meaningful form. It becomes the institution's academic DNA.

**Why it's a moat:** The graph is not a document. It is a dynamic, versioned, institution-specific knowledge structure that every feature in PrepareMe builds on top of. Losing it means losing the entire intelligence layer.

### Layer 2: Student Mastery Memory (Usage Moat)
Every student interaction — every quiz, every explanation request, every wrong answer, every revision session — is logged and used to update a persistent mastery model for that student.

Over time, the system knows not just what a student answered incorrectly, but what they consistently confuse, what they forget after two weeks, what they need to see three times before it sticks, and how their recovery speed changes under exam pressure.

**Why it's a moat:** This memory cannot be transferred. It is not a CSV export. It is a dynamic, time-series model of each student's learning history. Starting over means losing months of intelligence. Institutions will not accept that cost.

### Layer 3: Institutional Policy Engine (Compliance Moat)
Every institution configures PrepareMe with its specific answer policies: what sources are approved, how strict the hallucination firewall is, what explanation depth is appropriate for different student levels, what language style to use, and what content requires human approval before it reaches students.

**Why it's a moat:** These policies become embedded in operational workflows. Changing platforms means re-encoding all of these policies from scratch — a task that is both time-consuming and risky for compliance-conscious institutions.

### Layer 4: Teacher Workflows (Workflow Moat)
Teachers use PrepareMe to create quizzes, generate summaries, review AI-generated content, publish materials, and monitor student progress. Over time, these workflows become habits. The teacher's entire content library — structured, tagged, and version-controlled — lives inside PrepareMe.

**Why it's a moat:** Teachers who have used PrepareMe's Teacher Autopilot for six months have their entire curriculum organized, their quizzes generated and reviewed, and their student analytics integrated. Migrating this to another platform is not a technical problem — it is an organizational disruption that nobody wants to take on mid-year.

### Layer 5: Analytics and Outcome Data (Insight Moat)
PrepareMe generates longitudinal learning analytics: cohort performance trends, weak-topic clustering, exam readiness distributions, and content effectiveness scores. This data is only meaningful in aggregate and over time. It becomes a strategic asset for institutional leadership.

**Why it's a moat:** Administrators who have made curriculum decisions, hired faculty, or restructured programs based on PrepareMe analytics are deeply invested. This is not a tool. It is infrastructure.

### Layer 6: Network Effects (Eventual Moat)
As more institutions in a given exam category (e.g., JEE prep) onboard PrepareMe, the platform accumulates meta-intelligence: which question patterns appear across institutions, which concepts cause universal confusion, what remediation strategies work best. This meta-intelligence can be used to improve everyone's experience without sharing any tenant-specific data.

**Why it's a moat:** First-movers in each exam category benefit from this intelligence earliest. Latecomers start from zero.

## 6. Unique Features and Differentiation

### 6.1 Syllabus DNA Engine — MOAT-BUILDING
**What it is:** An ingestion and normalization pipeline that converts any curriculum asset (PDF syllabus, DOCX notes, PPTX lectures, past papers) into a canonical, machine-readable learning graph.

**How it works:** The system extracts topics, subtopics, and learning outcomes from uploaded materials. It maps relationships between them (prerequisites, co-requisites, exam weights). It detects gaps, overlaps, and drift when content is updated. The result is a versioned, queryable knowledge graph that is unique to each institution.

**Why it's a moat:** This graph is the institution's academic DNA. It takes weeks to build correctly. It is the foundation of every other feature. Without it, nothing else works. It is not a document — it is a data structure that gets richer with every upload.

**Classification: MOAT-BUILDING**

### 6.2 Learning Memory Layer — MOAT-BUILDING
**What it is:** A persistent, per-student memory system that tracks mastery, confusion, forgetting, and recovery across every topic in the curriculum graph.

**How it works:** Every student interaction updates a mastery model. The system tracks not just correctness but confidence, response time, consistency, and forgetting curves. Over time, it builds a learning fingerprint for each student that drives all future recommendations.

**Why it's a moat:** This memory cannot be transferred or replicated without the interaction history. It compounds over time. Students who have used PrepareMe for a semester have a learning history that informs every future session. Starting over on a new platform means losing that intelligence entirely.

**Classification: MOAT-BUILDING**

### 6.3 Hallucination Firewall — MOAT-BUILDING
**What it is:** A multi-stage retrieval and verification system that prevents the AI from generating answers that are not grounded in the institution's approved materials.

**How it works:**
1. Every query is first evaluated against the syllabus scope (is this topic in the curriculum?)
2. Retrieval is performed against the tenant's document corpus with confidence scoring
3. If retrieval confidence is below threshold, the system hedges, redirects, or refuses
4. If the answer is outside syllabus scope, the student is informed and offered to flag for teacher review
5. Every answer includes provenance metadata: which documents, which sections, what confidence level

**Why it's a moat:** Institutions cannot use generic AI tools because they cannot control what the AI says. The hallucination firewall is not a feature — it is the reason institutions can use AI at all. No competitor has built this at the institutional level with per-tenant policy controls.

**Classification: MOAT-BUILDING**

### 6.4 Exam Twin Engine — MOAT-BUILDING
**What it is:** A system that clones the exam style of each institution's specific exams — question patterns, difficulty distributions, phrasing conventions, recurring traps, and marking schemes — and generates practice exams that are indistinguishable in style from the real thing.

**How it works:** The system ingests historical past papers, analyzes question type distributions, difficulty curves, and topic coverage patterns, and builds an exam-style model for each course. When generating practice exams, it respects this style model rather than generating generic questions.

**Why it's a moat:** This is not a generic quiz generator. It requires historical past papers, institution-specific style analysis, and a trained style model per course. The longer an institution uses it, the better the style model becomes. Competitors starting from zero cannot replicate this without years of data.

**Classification: MOAT-BUILDING**

### 6.5 Socratic Viva Engine — OPERATIONALLY USEFUL
**What it is:** An interactive mode where the AI interrogates the student's understanding through follow-up questions, concept chaining, and misconception detection — rather than simply delivering answers.

**How it works:** When a student asks a question or states an answer, the system generates a follow-up probe that tests the depth of understanding. It detects surface-level pattern matching ("the student said the right word but can't apply it") and escalates to deeper probing. It logs misconception patterns and feeds them into the mastery model.

**Why it's useful:** Students often believe they understand something when they don't. The viva engine surfaces this gap before the exam. It is a qualitatively different experience from flashcards or MCQs.

**Classification: OPERATIONALLY USEFUL** (not a moat on its own — but differentiating in sales demos and becomes a retention lever when students experience "learning debt" being surfaced)

### 6.6 Auto-Remediation Engine — MOAT-BUILDING
**What it is:** When the mastery model detects a weak area, the system automatically generates a targeted remediation plan: micro-lessons, practice questions, flashcards, spaced repetition schedules, and worked examples — all grounded in the curriculum graph.

**How it works:** The mastery model identifies not just which topics are weak, but why they are weak (confusion with a related concept, prerequisite gap, forgetting after initial learning). The remediation plan addresses the root cause, not just the symptom. It is personalized to each student's learning history.

**Why it's a moat:** This requires the curriculum graph (to understand prerequisites), the mastery memory (to understand why the student is weak), and the retrieval system (to pull the right content). No single component is replicable in isolation. Together, they create a system that gets materially better with usage.

**Classification: MOAT-BUILDING**

### 6.7 Teacher Autopilot — OPERATIONALLY USEFUL
**What it is:** A workflow that turns raw uploads (lecture slides, notes, assignments) into organized institutional assets: syllabus-mapped content, generated quizzes, answer keys, topic summaries, and quality flags.

**How it works:** Teacher uploads a file. The system extracts content, maps it to the curriculum graph, identifies gaps or overlaps, generates quiz questions with difficulty tags, produces a summary, and flags content that may need review (outdated information, low confidence on alignment, missing learning outcomes).

**Why it's useful:** Teachers save 5–10 hours per week on administrative content work. Over time, the institution's entire content library is organized, versioned, and curriculum-mapped inside PrepareMe. This is a workflow moat — not because the feature itself is uncopyable, but because the content library built with it is.

**Classification: OPERATIONALLY USEFUL** (strong retention lever; the content library becomes the moat)

### 6.8 Outcome Mapper — OPERATIONALLY USEFUL
**What it is:** A system that maps every student interaction to Bloom's Taxonomy levels, learning outcomes, and exam readiness indicators.

**How it works:** Every quiz question, explanation request, and viva exchange is tagged with its Bloom's level (remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, create). The system tracks not just topic coverage but cognitive depth coverage. A student may have answered 50 questions about a topic at the "remember" level without ever demonstrating "apply" level mastery.

**Why it's useful:** This is the reporting layer that institutional leadership actually wants — not "the student answered 70% correctly" but "the cohort has not demonstrated application-level mastery of thermodynamics concepts before the exam."

**Classification: OPERATIONALLY USEFUL** (compliance and reporting value; not a primary moat but deeply sticky for administrators)

### 6.9 Institutional Policy Engine — MOAT-BUILDING
**What it is:** A configuration layer that allows each institution to define, in fine-grained detail, how the AI behaves for their students — answer strictness, content approval requirements, allowed sources, explanation depth, language, and assessment rules.

**How it works:** Administrators configure policies via a rule builder. Policies are applied at the retrieval, generation, and output layers. Policies can be scoped to courses, student levels, or content types. Changes take effect immediately and are logged in the audit trail.

**Why it's a moat:** These policies encode institutional knowledge, compliance requirements, and pedagogical philosophy. They are not transferable. They become embedded in the institution's operational culture. Changing platforms means re-encoding everything from scratch — and risking compliance gaps in the process.

**Classification: MOAT-BUILDING**

### 6.10 Learning Debt Meter — NICE-TO-HAVE (but retention lever)
**What it is:** A student-facing visualization that shows how much the student actually understands — not just what they've completed or answered correctly, but where the gaps in durable understanding are.

**How it works:** The mastery model computes a "learning debt" score per topic based on recency, consistency, application depth, and forgetting curves. Topics where the student answered correctly once a month ago but has not revisited are flagged as debt. Topics where the student consistently confuses one concept with another are flagged as structural debt.

**Why it's a retention lever:** Students who see their learning debt clearly become emotionally invested in the platform. It is not a moat against institutional switching, but it drives daily engagement and makes PrepareMe the student's primary study tool.

**Classification: NICE-TO-HAVE** (strong UX and engagement feature; not an independent moat but improves retention and NPS significantly)

### 6.11 Content Drift Alarm — OPERATIONALLY USEFUL
**What it is:** A system that detects when a curriculum asset has drifted from the current syllabus version — outdated content, questions referencing deprecated exam patterns, or explanations inconsistent with the latest approved materials.

**How it works:** When a new syllabus version is uploaded or an exam board updates its guidelines, the system compares existing content against the new version, flags conflicts, and surfaces them to teachers for review.

**Why it's useful:** In high-stakes exam preparation, outdated content is a liability. This alarm is a safety net that no generic AI tool provides.

**Classification: OPERATIONALLY USEFUL**

### 6.12 Class-Level Confusion Clustering — MOAT-BUILDING
**What it is:** An analytics feature that aggregates individual mastery signals to identify class-wide confusion patterns — not just which students are struggling, but which concepts are causing widespread confusion across the entire cohort.

**How it works:** The system clusters mastery signals across students. When a significant percentage of a cohort shows weak mastery, high confusion, or rapid forgetting on the same concept, it surfaces this as a class-level alert with suggested intervention (re-teach session, supplementary content, exam de-emphasis).

**Why it's a moat:** This requires aggregated, longitudinal mastery data across the cohort. It is not possible without the learning memory layer. It is a capability that institutional leadership genuinely values and cannot get anywhere else.

**Classification: MOAT-BUILDING**

### 6.13 Paper Style Cloning — MOAT-BUILDING
(Covered under Exam Twin Engine above — the style model per institution is the moat)

### 6.14 Multilingual Academic Explanations — NICE-TO-HAVE
**What it is:** The ability to generate explanations in the student's preferred language while maintaining academic precision.

**Why it's nice-to-have:** Important for Indian markets (Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Marathi, Bengali) but not a moat on its own. It is a market-access feature, not a defensibility feature.

**Classification: NICE-TO-HAVE** (required for Indian market penetration; not a moat)

## 8. Recommended Tech Stack

The principle: **monolith first, with clean internal boundaries. Extract services only at proven split points.**

### Backend
**Framework: Python with FastAPI**
- Rationale: Python dominates the AI/ML ecosystem. FastAPI is production-grade, async-native, and has excellent type support. It co-locates AI orchestration code with the API layer without requiring a separate service. Django is heavier than needed for MVP; Flask lacks structure. FastAPI is the right call.
- Internal modules: `curriculum/`, `retrieval/`, `assessment/`, `memory/`, `policies/`, `analytics/`, `auth/`, `admin/`
- Do NOT microservice these on day one. Internal boundaries via module interfaces are sufficient.

**When to extract services:**
- Ingestion pipeline: Extract as an async worker service at V1 when document processing volume exceeds synchronous capacity
- AI orchestration: Extract only if latency SLAs require dedicated scaling
- Analytics: Extract at V2 if query volume impacts transactional database performance

### Frontend
**Framework: Next.js 14+ (App Router) with TypeScript**
- Rationale: SSR for SEO (institution landing pages), RSC for performance, excellent TypeScript support, large ecosystem. React is the team skill base. Next.js is the pragmatic choice.
- UI components: shadcn/ui + Tailwind CSS — composable, accessible, fast to build with, no vendor lock-in
- State management: Zustand for client state; React Query / TanStack Query for server state
- No Redux. Overkill for this product at this stage.

### AI Orchestration
**Framework: LangChain (Python) for rapid prototyping → migrate to direct API calls + custom orchestration at V1**
- Rationale: LangChain is useful for standing up retrieval pipelines quickly. However, its abstractions become liabilities at scale (opaque behavior, debugging difficulty, version churn). By V1, replace the LangChain retrieval chain with a custom orchestration layer that gives full control over retrieval policy, reranking, confidence scoring, and prompt construction.
- The orchestration layer is where the hallucination firewall, confidence thresholds, and policy engine live. This must be code you own, not a black box.

### Retrieval Stack
**Primary: pgvector (PostgreSQL extension)**
- Rationale: Collocating vector search with relational data in a single PostgreSQL instance eliminates operational complexity at MVP and V1. pgvector handles millions of vectors with acceptable query latency. Only migrate to a dedicated vector database (Qdrant or Weaviate) when query volume or latency requirements justify it.
- Chunking strategy: Semantic chunking (not fixed-size) using a sliding window with overlap. Chunks are tagged with curriculum graph node IDs, enabling structured filtering before vector search.
- Reranking: Cross-encoder reranking (Cohere Rerank or a self-hosted cross-encoder model) on top of ANN retrieval results. This step significantly improves precision, which is critical for the hallucination firewall.

### Primary Database
**PostgreSQL 16+**
- Rationale: The curriculum graph, student mastery models, policy configurations, audit logs, and all relational data live in PostgreSQL. With pgvector, it also handles vector search. One database for MVP through V1 is the right call — it eliminates the operational burden of managing multiple database systems while the team is small.
- Schema: Multi-tenant with row-level security (RLS) policies. Each tenant has an `institution_id` on every table. RLS enforces zero cross-tenant data access at the database layer.

### Cache
**Redis (Upstash for serverless, self-hosted Redis for dedicated deployments)**
- Use cases: Session tokens, rate limiting, frequently accessed syllabus graph nodes, curriculum search result caching, student mastery read cache

### Queues
**Celery + Redis (MVP/V1) → migrate to BullMQ or AWS SQS at V2 if needed**
- Use cases: Document ingestion pipeline, quiz generation jobs, analytics computation, email notifications, spaced repetition scheduling
- Rationale: Celery with Redis is simple to operate and well-understood. Do not introduce a managed queue service until the volume justifies it.

### Object Storage
**AWS S3 (or Cloudflare R2 for cost at scale)**
- Use: Original uploaded documents, generated PDFs, exported reports, student submission files

### Key Architectural Decisions

**Decision 1: LLM Provider as Pluggable Adapter**
The LLM is accessed only through a provider adapter interface:
```python
class LLMProvider(Protocol):
    def complete(self, prompt: Prompt, config: GenerationConfig) -> LLMResponse: ...
    def embed(self, text: str) -> Embedding: ...
```
Switching from OpenAI to Anthropic to a self-hosted model requires only a new adapter implementation. The orchestration logic, retrieval policy, and confidence scoring are provider-agnostic.

**Decision 2: Curriculum Graph as First-Class Data Structure**
The curriculum graph is not a flat table. It is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) stored in PostgreSQL using a self-referential adjacency list with a materialized path for fast traversal. Each node carries: topic name, learning outcomes, Bloom's level, difficulty weight, exam weight, prerequisite links, and content provenance.

**Decision 3: Multi-Tenancy via Row-Level Security + Application Layer**
Every database query includes an `institution_id` predicate enforced by PostgreSQL RLS policies. The application layer sets the `institution_id` context at request initialization from the verified JWT. A middleware test runs in the CI pipeline that verifies no API endpoint can return data for a different institution than the authenticated user belongs to.

**Decision 4: Retrieval Policy is Configuration, Not Code**
The retrieval pipeline (scope check → vector search → rerank → confidence score → policy filter → generation) is driven by a per-tenant `RetrievalPolicy` configuration object. Teachers and admins configure this via the policy engine. A strict institution gets a higher confidence threshold and a narrower scope. A research university might allow broader scope with explicit out-of-scope flagging.

## 11. Vector and Retrieval Design

### Chunking Strategy

**Do not use fixed-size chunks.** Fixed-size chunking breaks semantic coherence and degrades retrieval precision, which is unacceptable for the hallucination firewall.

Use **semantic chunking**:
1. Extract text from documents, preserving section structure (headings, paragraphs, lists)
2. Identify semantic boundaries using a lightweight classifier
3. Create chunks of 300–600 tokens with 10% overlap at boundaries
4. Tag each chunk with: document ID, page number, section heading, curriculum node ID (if mappable), and a content hash

**Curriculum-node tagging during chunking** is critical. When a chunk is tagged with a curriculum node ID, retrieval can be filtered by curriculum scope before vector search, dramatically improving precision and speed.

### Retrieval Pipeline

**Stage 1 — Scope Check (< 5ms)**
Before any embedding or vector search, evaluate the query against the curriculum graph using a lightweight topic classifier. Determine: is this query within scope for the student's enrolled course? If outside scope, invoke refusal logic immediately without incurring embedding cost.

**Stage 2 — Hybrid Retrieval (< 100ms)**
Run two retrievals in parallel:
- Dense retrieval: Cosine similarity against tenant-scoped embeddings using pgvector
- Sparse retrieval: BM25 keyword search against chunk text using PostgreSQL full-text search (tsvector)
- Merge results using Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)

Why hybrid: Dense retrieval handles semantic similarity; sparse retrieval handles exact keyword matching (important for technical terms, formula names, and proper nouns in academic content). Neither alone is sufficient.

**Stage 3 — Curriculum-Graph-Aware Filtering (< 10ms)**
Filter merged results to retain only chunks whose curriculum node IDs are:
- Within the student's enrolled course, AND
- Permitted by the tenant's retrieval policy (some institutions restrict by unit or topic)

**Stage 4 — Cross-Encoder Reranking (< 200ms)**
Rerank filtered results using a cross-encoder model (Cohere Rerank API or a self-hosted BGE Reranker). Cross-encoders evaluate query-document pairs jointly, significantly improving precision over bi-encoder ANN retrieval alone.

**Stage 5 — Confidence Scoring (< 10ms)**
Compute a retrieval confidence score based on:
- Top reranked score
- Score gap between top and second results (high gap = high confidence)
- Number of chunks supporting the answer
- Curriculum node coverage (is the query topic well-represented in retrieved chunks?)

If confidence < tenant threshold: do not generate; invoke hedge or refusal logic.

**Stage 6 — Generation with Grounding (LLM call)**
Pass the top-k reranked chunks + confidence score + policy configuration to the LLM. The prompt instructs the model to answer only from the provided context, cite sources, and hedge explicitly when the provided context is insufficient.

**Stage 7 — Output Validation (< 20ms)**
After generation, validate the output:
- Does the response cite specific chunks from the retrieved context?
- Does the response length match the configured explanation depth?
- Does the response contain any flagged out-of-scope terms (configurable per institution)?
- Log the full retrieval + generation chain to `ai_interactions`

### Embedding Strategy

**Model:** `text-embedding-3-small` (OpenAI) for MVP — good quality/cost tradeoff. At V2, evaluate whether a domain-specific embedding model fine-tuned on academic content improves retrieval precision.

**Embedding updates:** Re-embed chunks whenever source documents are updated (versioning triggers re-embedding jobs via Celery). Do not re-embed the entire corpus on model updates — build an embedding version field and support gradual migration.

## 12. AI Prompt Architecture

### Prompt Layers

PrepareMe does not use a single system prompt. It uses a **layered prompt assembly system** where each layer is independently configurable:

**Layer 1 — Platform Identity**
Static, controlled by PrepareMe. Defines the AI's fundamental behavior: it is an academic assistant, not a general assistant; it answers only from provided context; it must cite sources; it must hedge when uncertain.

**Layer 2 — Institution Policy**
Dynamic, set by the institution. Defines answer strictness, explanation depth, language, and any institution-specific behavioral rules. Loaded from `tenant_policies` at request time.

**Layer 3 — Course Context**
Dynamic, set by course configuration. Includes the course name, enrolled unit, current topic, and relevant learning outcomes. Loaded from the curriculum graph.

**Layer 4 — Student Context**
Dynamic, computed from mastery memory. Includes the student's current mastery level for the relevant topic, recent weak areas, and any active remediation context. This personalizes the explanation depth and emphasis.

**Layer 5 — Retrieved Context**
Dynamic, produced by the retrieval pipeline. The actual chunks from approved documents, with source citations and confidence scores.

**Layer 6 — Task Specification**
Dynamic, set by the mode (explanation, quiz, viva, flashcard, revision). Defines the output format and behavioral expectations for the current interaction type.

### Prompt for Explanation Mode (Example)

```
[Layer 1 - Platform Identity]
You are an academic learning assistant for an educational institution. You answer questions
only from the approved curriculum materials provided below. You must cite your sources.
If the provided materials do not contain sufficient information to answer the question,
you must say so explicitly and do not speculate.

[Layer 2 - Institution Policy]
Institution: {institution_name}
Answer strictness: STRICT — do not include information not present in the provided materials.
Explanation depth: STANDARD — provide clear explanations without unnecessary elaboration.
Language: English (Indian academic standard)

[Layer 3 - Course Context]
Course: {course_name}
Current unit: {unit_name}
Current topic: {topic_name}
Learning objectives for this topic: {learning_outcomes}

[Layer 4 - Student Context]
Student mastery level for this topic: {mastery_score}/10
Recent weak areas: {weak_areas}
Adjust your explanation to address the student's current level.

[Layer 5 - Retrieved Context]
The following approved materials are relevant to the student's question.
Use only these materials to formulate your answer.

[Source 1 — {document_title}, Page {page_number}, Confidence: {confidence_score}]
{chunk_text}

[Source 2 — {document_title}, Page {page_number}, Confidence: {confidence_score}]
{chunk_text}

[Layer 6 - Task]
Answer the student's question below. Format your response as:
1. A direct answer (1–2 sentences)
2. An explanation with examples from the provided materials
3. A key point to remember
4. Cite the sources you used

Student question: {query}
```

### Refusal Logic

Three refusal modes, each with a different response:

**Refusal Type 1 — Out of Scope**
*Trigger:* Scope check detects query is outside enrolled curriculum
*Response:* "This topic appears to be outside your current course curriculum. If you believe this is an error, you can flag it for your teacher's review. [Flag for review]"

**Refusal Type 2 — Low Confidence**
*Trigger:* Retrieval confidence below threshold
*Response:* "I found some materials that may be relevant, but I'm not confident they fully address your question. Here's what I found: [partial answer with explicit uncertainty markers]. For a complete answer, please consult your teacher or the original source: [source citation]."

**Refusal Type 3 — No Relevant Content**
*Trigger:* No chunks retrieved above minimum similarity threshold
*Response:* "I wasn't able to find relevant material on this topic in the approved curriculum. This question has been flagged for your teacher's review. In the meantime, here are the closest related topics in your curriculum: [topic suggestions from curriculum graph]."

### Quiz Generation Prompt Strategy

Quiz generation uses a structured output prompt with JSON schema validation. The model is instructed to generate questions at specific Bloom's levels, covering specified curriculum nodes, at specified difficulty weights, in the institution's exam style format. Every generated question includes: question text, options (for MCQ), correct answer, explanation, curriculum node ID, Bloom's level, and difficulty weight.

Generated questions are never served to students directly. They enter an approval queue where teachers review, edit, and approve before publishing.

## 13. Multi-Tenancy and Security Model

### Tenant Isolation Architecture

**Logical isolation** (not physical isolation) is the approach for MVP through V2. Physical isolation (separate databases per tenant) is operationally expensive and only justified for enterprise contracts with specific data residency requirements.

Logical isolation is enforced at three independent layers:

**Layer 1 — Database (PostgreSQL RLS)**
Every table has an `institution_id` column. RLS policies enforce that every query returns only rows where `institution_id = current_setting('app.institution_id')`. The application sets this context variable at request initialization from the verified JWT. No query can ever return cross-tenant data without a programming error that bypasses JWT verification — which is independently tested.

**Layer 2 — Application Middleware**
Every API endpoint is wrapped in an `institution_context` middleware that:
1. Verifies the JWT
2. Extracts `institution_id` from claims
3. Sets the PostgreSQL session context variable
4. Validates that all resource IDs in the request belong to the authenticated institution

**Layer 3 — Vector Store (pgvector)**
All vector similarity searches include a mandatory `WHERE institution_id = ?` filter as a pre-filter before ANN search. No embedding lookup can cross tenant boundaries.

**Layer 4 — Object Storage (S3)**
Every S3 key is prefixed with `/{institution_id}/`. IAM policies on the application's S3 role restrict write access to the prefix matching the authenticated institution. Pre-signed URLs for document access are scoped to specific institution prefixes and expire in 1 hour.

### Security Policies

**Zero cross-tenant data access:** Verified by automated integration tests that create two tenant fixtures and assert that all API endpoints return 403 or empty results when accessed with the wrong tenant's credentials.

**Audit logging:** Every AI response, policy change, content approval/rejection, and role change is logged to `ai_interactions` or a dedicated `audit_log` table with full context. Audit logs are immutable (append-only, no update or delete permissions on the audit tables).

**Data encryption:** All data encrypted at rest (AWS encryption, PostgreSQL transparent data encryption). All data encrypted in transit (TLS 1.3 minimum). Embeddings in pgvector are encrypted at rest with the database.

**PII handling:** Student names, emails, and contact information are stored separately from learning data. Analytics and mastery records reference `student_id` (UUID), not PII. Exported analytics reports can be generated without PII by role policy.

**API key management:** Institution API keys (for B2B2C integrations at V2) are hashed and stored as `sha256(key)`. Keys are scoped to specific permissions and can be revoked per-institution.

**Penetration testing:** Scheduled quarterly from V1 onward. Findings addressed within defined SLA windows based on severity.

## 14. Evaluation and Quality Strategy

### AI Quality is Not Optional

The hallucination firewall is the product's primary trust mechanism. If it fails, institutions lose confidence and churn. Quality evaluation must be continuous, automated, and part of every deployment.

### Evaluation Framework

**Tier 1 — Unit Evaluation (runs on every commit)**
A test suite of ~500 curated Q&A pairs per exam category, with:
- Expected answer (or expected refusal)
- Expected source citations
- Expected confidence tier (high/medium/low)
- Expected bloom's level

Metrics measured: answer accuracy, citation precision, refusal recall (did the system correctly refuse when it should have?), false positive rate (did the system answer confidently when it shouldn't have?).

**Tier 2 — Retrieval Evaluation (runs on every deployment)**
Evaluate retrieval pipeline quality using a test curriculum corpus:
- Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) for relevant chunk retrieval
- Recall@K for key concept coverage
- Precision@K after reranking
- Latency at p50 and p95

Regression alerts if MRR or Recall@5 drops more than 5% relative to the previous deployment baseline.

**Tier 3 — Production Monitoring (continuous)**
Every AI response in production is:
- Logged with retrieval metadata and confidence score
- Randomly sampled for human review (5% sample rate, configurable)
- Flagged if confidence score is in the "uncertain" range for review

Teachers and students can flag responses as incorrect or unhelpful. Flagged responses are reviewed weekly and fed back into the evaluation test suite.

**Tier 4 — Exam Outcome Correlation (monthly)**
Where institutions share post-exam results, correlate PrepareMe's exam readiness scores with actual exam performance. This is the ultimate ground truth for whether the mastery model is calibrated. Correlations are computed per-institution and used to recalibrate the mastery model parameters.

### Content Quality Governance

Every piece of AI-generated content that will be served to students (quiz questions, flashcards, summaries) goes through a content approval workflow:
1. AI generates content with confidence score and curriculum node citations
2. Content enters a review queue for the responsible teacher
3. Teacher reviews, edits if needed, and approves or rejects
4. Approved content is published to students; rejected content is logged with reason
5. High-confidence, consistently approved content types can be configured for auto-approval by institution admins (with audit trail)

This human-in-the-loop workflow is not a product limitation. It is a governance feature that institutions will pay for — it is what makes PrepareMe defensible against liability concerns.

## 17. Risks and Mitigations

### Risk 1 — Sales Cycle Length
**Risk:** Institutional sales cycles are long (3–6 months), and revenue is slow to materialize.
**Mitigation:** Lead with a self-serve free tier and a 30-day pilot to compress the evaluation cycle. The free tier generates usage data and creates internal champions who drive the purchase decision from within the institution.

### Risk 2 — Curriculum Graph Build Time
**Risk:** Building the curriculum graph for a new institution requires significant data wrangling and may take longer than expected, delaying time to value.
**Mitigation:** Build a Teacher Autopilot that makes the initial upload-and-map flow as fast as possible. Offer a concierge onboarding service (human-assisted graph building) for enterprise contracts. Set clear onboarding SLAs with consequences.

### Risk 3 — Hallucination Firewall Failure
**Risk:** A high-profile hallucination event damages trust with a key institution.
**Mitigation:** The content approval workflow is mandatory for exam-facing content. Confidence thresholds are set conservatively by default. All AI responses in audit logs. Post-incident review process with root cause analysis and public disclosure policy.

### Risk 4 — LLM Provider Disruption
**Risk:** A key LLM provider increases prices, degrades quality, or discontinues the API.
**Mitigation:** The provider adapter interface makes switching providers a week of engineering work, not a rewrite. Maintain active relationships with at least two providers (OpenAI and Anthropic). Evaluate open-source model viability (Llama, Mistral) quarterly. The product's moat does not depend on a specific model.

### Risk 5 — Incumbent Response
**Risk:** Google, Microsoft, or Canvas bolt on a curriculum-aware AI feature to their existing platforms.
**Mitigation:** Incumbents move slowly in enterprise education. Their "AI features" will be shallow for years. PrepareMe's moat is the curriculum graph, the mastery memory, and the institutional data — none of which incumbents can replicate quickly. Focus on deepening these moats and winning contracts before incumbents catch up.

### Risk 6 — Data Privacy and Regulation
**Risk:** Student data regulations (FERPA in US, DPDP Act in India) create compliance complexity.
**Mitigation:** Build privacy-by-design from day one: RLS isolation, PII separation, audit logs, data retention policies, and explicit data processing agreements with institutions. Hire a compliance-aware legal advisor at Series A. DPDP compliance in India is a competitive advantage, not just a burden — it differentiates PrepareMe from casual API wrappers.

### Risk 7 — Retention Risk if Pass Rates Don't Improve
**Risk:** If exam pass rates don't measurably improve, institutions will not renew.
**Mitigation:** Set honest expectations upfront. Track engagement metrics that precede pass rate improvement (study hours, quiz completion, mastery score improvement). Correlate PrepareMe engagement with historical pass rate data from design partners. Build the Exam Risk Prediction model to give institutions a leading indicator, not just a lagging one.
